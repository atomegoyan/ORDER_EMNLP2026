\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We address five questions:
\textbf{RQ1} : does query-conditioned chunking optimisation improve retrieval over a global baseline?
\textbf{RQ2} : does query-conditioned \emph{metadata indexing} (filters and rerankers on \textit{Les Débats}) improve retrieval over a uniform global baseline?
\textbf{RQ3} : does the \emph{joint} optimisation of chunking and metadata yield gains beyond either single-axis variant, and do the two axes interact?
\textbf{RQ4} : does query-conditioned source selection improve retrieval over a single-index baseline?
\textbf{RQ5} : are query routing (QRe) and uniform multi-source allocation (UMS) complementary remedies for size-imbalanced retrieval?
\subsection{Task/Query-Conditioned Chunking}
\label{sec:indexing-results}

We analyse whether per-cluster selection of a chunking strategy improves
retrieval over a single global best. The comparison of the 14 candidate
strategies under the four retrievers is in Appendix~\ref{subsec:appendix-global-strategy-comparison}
(Table~\ref{tab:recall-coverage-k3}).
The realised gain on the held-out test set, decomposed by cluster and by
assigned strategy, is reported in
Tables~\ref{tab:chunking-wins-test-cluster} and~\ref{tab:chunking-wins-summary}
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:appendix-cluster-wins});
strategy-level qualitative wins are discussed in
Appendix~\ref{sec:strategy-wins}. A non-trivial subset of clusters
benefits from a strictly different strategy than the global baseline,
motivating the held-out transfer evaluation below.

\paragraph{Transfer to unseen queries (RQ1).}
The oracle analysis shows the potential for strong retrieval gains when
the appropriate chunking strategy is chosen, but it assumes the best
strategy is known in advance. We test the realised gain using the
inference procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:indexing-inference}.
% The cluster-based pipeline (Algorithm~\ref{alg:tc-inference}) is evaluated
% on the held-out 50\% test split (averaged over 5 random seeds), applying
% the strategy map $s^*$ learned on the training split to unseen queries
% via nearest-centroid assignment in UMAP space.
Results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:tc-inference-merged} (rows
\textit{+\,TC-Chunking}) alongside the metadata- and joint-axis variants
discussed below. Under the Naive Retriever the inference pipeline realises
$+5.4$~pp Coverage@3 and $+5.3$~pp Recall@3 ($p<0.05$), confirming
that a single low-cost classification step at query time delivers
meaningful gain. Gains are negligible and non-significant once routing
is active (at most $+0.2$~pp with UMS; $p>0.05$ for all routing
configurations), indicating that task-conditioned chunking and source
routing address entirely overlapping failure modes: the gains chunking
provides for the naive retriever are fully recovered by routing alone.

\paragraph{Mechanism: why the gain vanishes under routing.}
Decomposing the $+5.4$~pp Naive-Retriever Coverage@3 gain by gold
location shows it is carried entirely by the 36\% of questions whose
gold lies only in the newspapers ($+16.2$~pp), while questions with
gold in \textit{Les D\'ebats} see $-0.5$~pp. TC-Chunking therefore helps
not by improving \textit{D\'ebats} retrieval but by making \textit{D\'ebats}
chunks less competitive in the top-$k$, freeing slots for the true
newspaper gold. QRe achieves the same outcome more directly --- filtering
\textit{D\'ebats} out of the candidate pool whenever the classifier
predicts a newspaper-only question --- which is why the TC-Chunking
$\Delta$ collapses to $\leq 0.2$~pp once routing is active. Restricted
to the subset where TC-Chunking can still mechanically act
(gold in \textit{Les D\'ebats} \emph{and} \textit{Les D\'ebats} kept by
the router), $\Delta$ Coverage@3 is $-0.002\pm0.002$, and varying the
strategy-selection score-$k$ from 3 to 9 changes it by at most $\pm 0.001$.
Full decomposition and a residual-gain analysis are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-tc-decomposition}.

\subsection{Task-Conditioned Metadata and joint optimisation}
\label{sec:metadata-results}

Applying the same protocol to the orthogonal metadata axis
(Section~\ref{sec:metadata-methodology}), we hold chunking fixed at
\texttt{10000\_hierarchical} and let each cluster pick its best metadata
strategy on \textit{Les Débats}. The realised gain on the held-out test set, decomposed by cluster and by
assigned strategy, is reported in
Tables~\ref{tab:metadata-wins-test-cluster} and~\ref{tab:metadata-wins-test-strategy}
(Appendix~\ref{subsec:appendix-metadata-wins}).

\paragraph{Transfer to unseen queries (RQ2).}

Table~\ref{tab:tc-inference-merged} reports
Coverage@$k$ and Recall@$k$ across the four retrievers (rows
\textit{+\,TC-Metadata}). Unlike chunking, the metadata gain persists under UMS: the gain is
largest under the Naive Retriever ($+8.9$~pp Coverage@3, $+9.0$~pp
Recall@3; $p<0.05$), remains substantial under UMS ($+6.1$~pp Cov@3,
$+6.0$~pp Rec@3; $p<0.05$) where parliamentary chunks occupy a
guaranteed retrieval quota and filtering noisy document types directly
improves that quota, vanishes under QRe ($0.0$~pp, ns) which already
pre-selects away from noisy sources, and is absent under
QRe\,\&\,UMS ($0.0$~pp, ns).

\subsection{Task-Conditioned Joint Indexing}
\label{sec:joint-results}

Combining both axes (Section~\ref{sec:joint-methodology}), each cluster
selects its best pair $(c^{*}, m^{*})$ from the $14{\times}14{=}196$
Cartesian grid; assignment to held-out queries follows the same
nearest-centroid rule. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:joint-wins-summary} lists the eleven joint pairs picked by at least one of the 21 training clusters under the Naive
Retriever, together with the number of held-out test questions routed
to each pair at inference time. 

\paragraph{Transfer to unseen queries (RQ3).}
The joint-axis results are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:tc-inference-merged} (rows \textit{+\,TC-Joint}),
comparing the global baseline pair to the task-conditioned joint pair on
the held-out test set ($n_{\text{test}}{=}438$). Task conditioning helps under the Naive Retriever and UMS, but the
joint variant does not strictly dominate the single-axis alternatives.
Under the Naive Retriever the joint pair adds $+8.8$~pp Coverage@3 and
$+8.6$~pp Recall@3 ($p<0.05$), marginally below TC-Metadata
($+8.9$ / $+9.0$~pp); under UMS gains are $+6.0$~pp Cov@3 /
$+6.0$~pp Rec@3 ($p<0.05$), matching TC-Metadata. QRe and
QRe\,\&\,UMS show no significant improvement under any indexing
variant (ns). The absence of a strict joint advantage over single-axis
variants reflects a winner's-curse effect from the larger
$14{\times}14$ search space: selecting the best of 196 pairs on limited
training clusters introduces estimation noise that can offset the
theoretical benefit of jointly optimising both axes simultaneously.

\textit{newspaper $\rightarrow$ Débats} (571 q., one newspaper plus one
parliamentary document). HippoRAGv2~\cite{hipporagv2} is included as a
graph-based external baseline.

\paragraph{Overall (RQ4, RQ5).}
All three RAG configurations beat the Naive and HippoRAGv2 baselines at
every cut-off. The combined QRe\,\&\,UMS reaches Recall@3 of 52.3
($+16.4$ pts over Naive, $+46\%$ rel.), with the gap widening at $k{=}10$.

\paragraph{Performances by question type.}
The two strategies fix different failures. On \emph{cross-newspaper}
questions, the Naive Retriever collapses (14.3 @3) as \textit{Les Débats}
floods the top-$k$ with parliamentary chunks unrelated to the query; QRe
removes this contamination and triples Recall@3 (48.4), while UMS alone
is less effective (40.8) because it still reserves a third of the budget
for \textit{Les Débats}. On \emph{newspaper $\rightarrow$ Débats} questions
the asymmetry reverses: the Naive Retriever already performs well (47.8),
QRe is neutral (the routing map keeps all three sources active), and UMS
delivers the main lift (55.4) by guaranteeing parliamentary representation
that would otherwise be crowded out.

\paragraph{Complementarity (RQ5).}
Neither routing alone nor uniform allocation alone accounts for the full
gain: each component contributes independently, and their combination is
strictly dominant on every metric. QRe and UMS target structurally distinct
failure modes : source \emph{contamination} versus source \emph{starvation} that can co-occur on a single question, which is why combining them
dominates either component in isolation across both question types.


\paragraph{Comparison to other methods.}
We include BM25~\cite{bm25} as a sparse-retrieval baseline using the same chunking
as the Naive Retriever. BM25 underperforms the dense baselines on every
cut-off and on both question types, with the gap most pronounced on
\emph{cross-newspaper} questions (4.6 vs.\ 14.3 Recall@3 against the
Naive Retriever). Lexical matching is particularly ill-suited to this
benchmark: questions are paraphrased rather than quoted from the
articles, and the OCR'd 19th-century newspaper text introduces surface
variation (orthographic and typographic) that dense embeddings absorb
but term-frequency matching does not. We therefore retain dense
retrieval as the underlying index for all S-RAG variants. For HippoRAGv2, we used the embeddings model and LLM as in the other experiments. Despite its graph-based index, HippoRAGv2 trails the Naive Retriever on
cross-newspaper questions (11.6 vs.\ 14.3 @3) and falls below all S-RAG
variants on every metric, suggesting graph propagation struggles when
evidence is spread across heterogeneous corpora with markedly different
registers.
